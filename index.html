How to Specify I-Joist Floor Systems

A useful I-joist specification defines outcomes and responsibilities without pretending every design decision belongs in a product paragraph. It aligns structural drawings, delegated engineering, submittals, installation instructions, and field quality so bidders understand both the member criteria and the completed floor assembly.

Independent technical explainer · 11 November 2026

ABCD

01

Write measurable performance criteria

Identify design loads, deflection limits, vibration expectations, fire or acoustic assembly requirements, service conditions, and applicable standards. State whether joist design, layout, connectors, and special details are by the engineer of record, supplier, or delegated designer. Ambiguous responsibility creates duplicated assumptions or gaps. Criteria should match the loading and supports shown on the structural documents.

02

Control substitutions by equivalence

An alternate joist depth or series can change reactions, hole zones, hanger selections, fire listings, floor feel, and service coordination. A substitution review therefore needs more than an allowable-span comparison. Require revised calculations, layout, details, connector schedule, assembly evidence, and a list of impacts to other disciplines. Set the review timing early enough that a valid alternate can be coordinated without delaying fabrication.

03

Make submittals useful

Request current product data, layout drawings, design calculations where required, installation guides, hole charts, connector information, and condition-specific details. Submittals should identify loads or support information provided by the design team and flag unresolved interfaces. A coordinated digital sheet is more valuable than a stack of generic literature. Review comments should distinguish design corrections from field coordination items.

04

Close out the information chain

The final record should include approved layouts, product identifiers, repair details, significant field changes, and maintenance cautions. Owners and future renovators need to know where openings may be added and whom to contact for evaluation. A clear archive reduces the chance that a later trade treats an engineered web like an unrestricted chase.
